## Scenario

Diane, a new graduate, joins a prestigious augmented reality research team. Max is its celebrated technical lead, but he humiliates staff over mistakes. Jean, the research manager, protects him. Before a demonstration, Diane discovers a defect involving coworker Eli's work. Later, unfair allocation of research credit forces her to consider whether to challenge leadership, support Eli, make a report to HR adviser Priya, stay, transfer, or leave.

The central issue is abuse of professional power and institutional complicity. The technical problem provides pressure; it must not displace the ethical focus.

## Characters

Diane: player, new graduate, values growth and financial stability but is concerned about harm and fairness.

Max: technical lead, prioritizes prestige and deadlines and uses his authority abusively. His accomplishments explain his power, not the acceptability of his conduct.

Jean: research manager, fears loss of funding, technical leadership, and reputation. She dismisses Diane's initial concern. Use this name and role consistently; Ada is a drafting inconsistency.

Eli: coworker, wants reliable work and fair credit, fears retaliation, and has his own preferences about participating in a complaint. Give him substantive dialogue.

Priya: HR adviser, explains this fictional company's reporting and support options. She gathers accounts but cannot guarantee a particular outcome. Give her substantive dialogue.

## Decision stages and rationales

### 1. First outburst

- Tell Jean: seek supervisory intervention; learn that management protects Max.
- Record the event: keep a factual account and relevant material Diane is authorized to access, improving the specificity of any later report.
- Stay quiet for now: protect immediate career stability, but receive no immediate institutional support.

These routes may rejoin at the project milestone. Later text must remember what actually happened.

### 2. Defect before the demonstration

- Ask Eli to review and test a fix together: build confidence and trust, with a possible schedule cost.
- Attempt a rushed repair alone: avoid exposing Eli initially but introduce a regression because review was inadequate.
- Raise the defect openly: make the risk visible; Max directs blame at Eli, creating a chance for Diane to respond.

A coding mistake does not justify abuse. The reporting employee is not responsible for Max's choice to mistreat a colleague.

### 3. Credit and coworker support

- Challenge the authorship decision using contribution evidence: protect earned credit despite risk of further hostility.
- Support Eli privately and ask what he wants shared: respect agency and privacy while considering next steps.
- Accept the decision for now: protect short-term stability but leave unfair recognition unresolved.

Diane may describe her own experiences and observations even if Eli declines to provide a statement. Do not imply she must obtain his permission to report her own treatment.

### 4. Longer-term response

- Make a factual formal complaint to Priya.
- Request a transfer or leave, with a possible factual exit account.
- Stay and build safer peer practices.
- Continue without challenging leadership.

Make the tradeoffs understandable. Do not present reporting, staying, or leaving as a mechanically correct or cowardly answer.

## Endings

E1 - Accountability begins: corroborated information leads to review, corrected credit, and an experienced interim supervisor. Reform takes time; the project faces delay and damaged trust.

E2 - Review unresolved: available information does not settle every allegation. Interim support or a transfer is arranged while review continues. This is not proof that Diane's experience was false.

E3 - A safer exit: Diane transfers or leaves, protecting her well-being but losing the particular opportunity. The wider team problem remains unresolved.

E4 - Local progress: peer support and technical review improve immediate conditions, while Jean's protection of Max continues.

E5 - Success with a cost: the milestone is achieved but fear and unfair credit persist. If the selected route caused a regression, explicitly show its later repair, delay, or other coherent consequence before claiming success.

Use branch-sensitive details, brief ACM reflections, and restart controls. Do not require fake moral scores, random punishment, or legal certainty.

## Learning goals

1. Distinguish constructive professional criticism from abuse.
2. Compare self-protection, support for coworkers, and reporting under uncertainty.
3. Explain why mistakes do not justify removing earned research credit.
4. Explain leadership's effect on working conditions and technical quality.
